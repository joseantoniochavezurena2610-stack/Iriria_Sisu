Monthly climate

How it is calculated and what still needs validation

Each year is split into three four-month seasons. For every crop and season a fit from 0 to 100 % is computed: mean temperature against the crop's optimal range, water balance (effective rainfall + available irrigation against the cycle's need), excess rain by drainage, and pH. A beam search builds 9-cycle sequences and scores them on four axes: soil (nitrogen, cover, residue, family diversity, repetitions), water (how much water beyond rain it needs), income (relative margin × fit, with a bonus after a legume and a penalty for repeating a family) and resilience (the same plan with 25 % less rain and +1.5 °C).

Crop parameters are starting values within the typical FAO-56, FAO-33 and ECOCROP ranges; they must be checked one by one and adjusted with INTA technical sheets. The margin is a relative index from 0 to 5, not colones. The water balance is monthly and simple: it does not replace a crop model.

The farmer first, then the satellite

The challenge asks for a decision-support tool. Teams that lose build a map viewer; teams that win solve a concrete decision. Here the decision is a single one: what do I plant in each season over the next few years. Everything you research has to feed that answer.

  • Talk to 3 to 5 people before the hackathon: a staple-grain grower, a vegetable grower, and an extension agent from MAG or INTA. Ask how they decide what to plant today, who advises them, what went wrong in the last dry year, and whether they have a soil test.
  • Find out which soil data a farmer really has. Almost nobody knows their organic-matter percentage. So the tool must accept simple answers (texture by feel, colour, whether it waterlogs) and fill in the rest with SoilGrids.
  • Write down exact quotes. A real quote from a grower in the video is worth more than any chart.

Agronomy the model must respect

Rotation rules

  • Do not repeat a botanical family in consecutive cycles: it breaks pest and soil-disease cycles.
  • A legume before a crop that takes up a lot of nitrogen (maize, potato, vegetables).
  • Never leave bare soil in the heavy-rain season on sloping land.
  • Alternate deep and shallow roots.
  • Green manures (mucuna, crotalaria) in the season that cannot support a cash crop anyway.

What to read

  • FAO-56: crop coefficients (Kc) and reference evapotranspiration.
  • FAO-33: yield response to water deficit (Ky factor).
  • ECOCROP: temperature, rainfall and pH ranges per species.
  • How El Niño affects Costa Rica's Pacific slope (less rain) versus the Caribbean.
  • Projects from the 2022–2025 Space Apps agriculture challenges, to avoid repeating what has been done.

How to show that it works

  1. Backtest. Take a known dry year in Guanacaste, download that year's POWER rainfall and the NDVI series of a grain area. If the model says “low fit” and NDVI also dropped, you have a validation almost no team presents.
  2. Expert check. Show three plans to an extension agent and ask which one they would plant and which one is nonsense. Fix the catalog with what they say.
  3. Sensitivity test. Change the pH or texture and check that the plan changes in the logical direction. If it does not change, that field is not needed in the form.

The proposal in one sentence

“In two minutes and without fast internet, a farmer gets three 3-year rotations made for their plot, with the reason behind each one and how well each plan holds up in a dry year.”

What sets you apart: comparing against what the grower does today, explaining every recommendation in farm language, and showing the plan's weak spot instead of hiding it.

Risks worth saying out loud

  • POWER's resolution is coarse (tens of kilometres). In mountainous areas temperature must be corrected for altitude.
  • The market matters as much as the climate. Without real prices, the income axis is only indicative.
  • A wrong recommendation costs someone a harvest. The tool suggests and explains; it does not command.
